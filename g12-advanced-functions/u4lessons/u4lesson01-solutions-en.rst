Part 1: What is a Radian?
================================================================================

Angles are commonly measured in :sol:`degrees`. However, in mathematics and
physics, there are many applications in which expressing an angle as a pure
number, without units, is more convenient than using degrees.

When measuring in :sol:`radians`, the size of an angle is expressed in terms
of the length of an arc, :math:`a`, that subtends the angle, :math:`\theta`,
where :math:`\theta = \dfrac{a}{r}`.

.. image:: ../images/u4lesson01-gpimage01.png
   :scale: 60
   :alt: circle showing radius r, arc a, and angle theta, where theta = a/r

1 Radian is defined as the size of an angle that is subtended by an arc with
a length :sol:`equal to the radius` of the circle.

Therefore, when the arc length and radius are equal, :solmath:`\theta =
\dfrac{a}{r} = \dfrac{r}{r} = 1 \text{ radian}`

.. image:: ../images/u4lesson01-gpimage02.png
   :scale: 60
   :alt: circle showing a = r, theta = 1 radian

How many radians are in a full circle? Or in other words, how many times can
an arc length equal to the radius fit around the circumference of a circle?

Remember: :math:`C = 2\pi r`

So if we use the full circumference of the circle for the arc length,
:solmath:`\theta = \dfrac{a}{r} = \dfrac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi \text{ radians}`

.. image:: ../images/u4lesson01-gpimage03.png
   :scale: 60
   :alt: circle with circumference a = 2 pi r, labeled 360 degrees

Part 2: Switching Between Degrees and Radians
================================================================================

The key relationship you need to know in order to switch between degrees
and radians is:

:math:`360° = 2\pi \text{ radians}`

.. list-table::
   :header-rows: 0
   :widths: 50 50

   * - 1 degree = :solmath:`\dfrac{\pi}{180}` radians

       To switch from degrees to radians, multiply by

       :solmath:`\dfrac{\pi}{180}`
     - 1 radian = :solmath:`\dfrac{180}{\pi}` degrees

       To switch from radians to degrees, multiply by

       :solmath:`\dfrac{180}{\pi}`

Part 3: Application
================================================================================

.. rst-class:: keepwithnext

**Example 4:** Suzette chooses a camel to ride on a carousel. The camel is
located 9 m from the center of the carousel. If the carousel turns through
an angle of :math:`\dfrac{5\pi}{6}`, determine the length of the arc
travelled by the camel, to the nearest tenth of a meter.

.. math::
   :nowrap:
   :class: solution

   \begin{array}{rcl}
   \theta &=& \dfrac{a}{r} \\
   a &=& \theta r \\
   a &=& \dfrac{5\pi}{6}(9) \\
   a &\cong& 23.6 \text{ m}
   \end{array}

Also, you will need to remember the UNIT CIRCLE, which is a circle that has
a radius of 1. Use the unit circle to write expressions for :math:`\sin
\theta`, :math:`\cos \theta`, and :math:`\tan \theta` in terms of
:math:`x`, :math:`y`, and :math:`r`.

.. image:: ../images/u4lesson01-gpimage10.png
   :scale: 60
   :alt: unit circle showing point (x,y) at angle theta, with x, y, and radius 1 labeled

On the unit circle, the sine and cosine functions take a simple form:

:solmath:`\sin \theta = y`

:solmath:`\cos \theta = x`

The value of :math:`\sin \theta` is the :sol:`y-coordinate` of each point on
the unit circle

The value of :math:`\cos \theta` is the :sol:`x-coordinate` of each point on
the unit circle

Therefore, we can use the points where the terminal arm intersects the unit
circle to get the sine and cosine ratios just by looking at the :math:`x`
and :math:`y` co-ordinates of the points.

:solmath:`\sin \theta = \dfrac{y}{r} = \dfrac{y}{1} = y`

:solmath:`\cos \theta = \dfrac{x}{r} = \dfrac{x}{1} = x`

:math:`\tan \theta = \dfrac{y}{x}`
